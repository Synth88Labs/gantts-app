What's included

A go-to-market plan fails when marketing, product, sales and pricing move on different clocks. This template puts every workstream on one timeline, in the six stages a launch actually moves through:

  • Market & customer research, Define the ideal customer profile and personas, analyse the competition, and validate the problem and willingness to pay before committing to a position. Milestone: research complete.
  • Positioning & messaging, Turn the research into a differentiated position and value proposition, then a messaging framework the whole team can use. Milestone: messaging approved.
  • Pricing & packaging, Decide the pricing model, tiers and packaging, and build the business case and revenue targets behind them. Milestone: pricing signed off.
  • Enablement & assets, Enable and train sales, build the website and landing pages, and produce the demo, collateral and documentation the launch needs. Milestone: assets ready.
  • Launch, Run the internal launch and readiness check, go live externally, and fire the campaigns, PR and sales outreach together. Milestone: external launch.
  • Post-launch & iterate, Measure the funnel and KPIs, gather win/loss feedback, and optimise, a launch is the start of the plan, not the end. Milestone: 30-day review.

Who uses this template

Product marketers, product managers and founders use this plan to launch a product without marketing, sales, product and pricing drifting out of sync. It suits B2B and SaaS teams taking a new product, feature or market entry from research through positioning and enablement to launch and the post-launch review.

Positioning is the dependency everything else inherits. Enablement, the website and the sales pitch all encode your positioning, so approve it as a hard milestone before those assets are built, reworking them after launch because the message changed is the most expensive GTM mistake.

How to customize it

  1. Set the launch date first and work backwards; enablement and asset production almost always need more runway than planned.
  2. Add a row per channel under Launch (paid, content, PR, partnerships) so each has its own owner and timing.
  3. If you are pre-launching to a waitlist or beta, pull the website and sign-ups earlier and gate the full launch behind readiness.
  4. Keep pricing on its own track, it depends on research, not on messaging, so it can run in parallel with positioning.
  5. Mark research, messaging, pricing and “assets ready” as milestones so no downstream workstream starts before its inputs exist.

Scheduling tips

  • Positioning is on the critical path. Messaging, enablement and every asset depend on it; a week lost approving positioning pushes the whole launch, so protect it.
  • Enablement is usually the bottleneck. Sales training, collateral and the demo take longer than expected and gate the launch, start them the moment messaging is approved.
  • Run pricing in parallel, not in series. It only needs the research, so overlapping it with positioning buys weeks back on the schedule.
  • Separate internal launch from external launch. The team needs to be ready before the market hears anything; schedule the internal go-live as its own milestone.
  • Schedule the post-launch review. The 30 days after launch, funnel metrics, win/loss, iteration, are where a plan compounds or quietly stalls for lack of follow-through.

Frequently asked questions

What is a go-to-market (GTM) plan?

A go-to-market plan is the timeline and workstreams that take a product to market, research, positioning, pricing, enablement, launch and post-launch. This template lays those stages on one schedule so marketing, product and sales stay in sync.

What should a go-to-market plan include?

At minimum: ideal customer profile and research, positioning and messaging, pricing and packaging, sales enablement and assets, the launch itself, and a post-launch measurement loop. The template gives each its own phase and milestones.

Can I show dependencies between GTM workstreams?

Yes. Open the template in the free gantts.app editor to link finish-to-start relationships (research → positioning → enablement → launch) so downstream work shifts automatically and the critical path to launch stays visible.
